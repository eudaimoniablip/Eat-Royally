**Chapter 4: Reservations**
---
The morning sun over Maui was relentless, flooding the master suite with a brilliant, sterile light. Arthur Woo sat at the edge of the teakwood bed after dressing. He looked the same, liver spots, thinning white hair, the tremor in his jaw, with the exception of the endlessly hungry-black hole behind his eyes. The baseline aggression that had defined his seventy years of existence, the ruthless dopaminergic drive that required him to conquer and hoard, was simply gone. It was as if someone had reached into his brain and took his ‘dragon’s hoard’ whilst laying upon it.


Yeongon sat at the dining table in a single end high-backed chair, sipping black tea. She looked immaculate. Her skin seemed to hold a faint, pearlescent vitality that hadn't been there the day before. One down.


“Arthur,” she said, her voice a soothing, melodic hum that held none of the terrifying resonance from the night before. “How are you feeling?” She motioned that he come over to the dining table and have tea with her.


Arthur looked down at his trembling hands. A single tear tracked through the deep wrinkles of his cheek and hung at the precipice of his chin before tapping Arthur’s pajama bottom covered knee. He stood and walked over sitting in the chair next to her.


“I… I don't know,” Arthur whispered, his voice thin and hollow like dry paper tearing. “I was thinking about the…pensions. The ones we liquidated in ’08 to cover the margin calls.”


Arthur wasn’t really speaking to Yeongon. He was staring blankly at the teakwood floor, addressing an invisible judge that was currently tearing through his psyche. For fifty years, Arthur Woo had utilized his immense wealth as a localized anesthetic. He had built towering, impenetrable walls of cognitive dissonance that allowed him to view human lives as nothing more than red and black ink on a quarterly ledger.


With a single, three-note whistle, Yeongon had systematically dismantled those walls. She hadn't just removed his armor; she had peeled back his skin and dropped him directly into the raw, agonizing nerve center of the collateral damage he had caused.


His mind violently snapped backward, dragging him out of the sun-drenched Hawaiian villa and dropping him into the freezing, air-conditioned chill of the Seoul boardroom eighteen years ago. He remembered the spreadsheet, it had been projected onto a massive plasma screen. The global markets were imploding, and Woo Telecom was facing a catastrophic liquidity crisis and his CFO had handed him a leather-bound folder outlining a four-hundred-million-dollar shortfall. The solution proposed was clinical, clean, and entirely sociopathic: raid the employee pension fund of a recently acquired manufacturing subsidiary.


Arthur remembered holding his heavy Montblanc fountain pen. He remembered being slightly annoyed that his coffee had gone cold. He remembered signing his name. The entire physical action had taken exactly three seconds.


Now, sitting on the edge of the teakwood bed, Arthur’s newly awakened prefrontal cortex was violently forced to process the downstream physics of those three seconds.


The numbers on the spreadsheet suddenly mutated, transforming into a torrential, sensory overload of human ruin. It hit Arthur’s nervous system like a localized nuclear detonation. He didn't just remember what he did; he felt it.


He felt the cold, clammy sweat of a sixty-two-year-old machinist standing at an ATM, watching the screen display a zero balance, realizing the safety net he had spent forty years building had simply evaporated overnight.


He felt the sharp, agonizing burn of shame in a father’s chest as he sat at a kitchen table, looking at the stack of unpaid utility bills, knowing he had to tell his daughter she could no longer afford university. Arthur tasted the bitter, metallic tang of cheap soju and quiet desperation. He felt the coarse, rough texture of the foreclosure notices taped to thousands of front doors. He heard the muffled, desperate arguments of marriages fracturing under the crushing hydrostatic pressure of sudden, unavoidable poverty.


Arthur gasped, his hands flying up to clutch his chest as his heart hammered erratically against his ribs. The pain wasn't physical; it was the sheer, suffocating weight of four thousand, two hundred families crashing down onto his fragile, seventy-year-old shoulders all at once.


“Thousands of families,” Arthur choked out, his chest heaving with a sudden, paralyzing panic.


He saw the faces. He saw the old men returning to manual labor with failing joints. He saw the suicides—the quiet, desperate men who walked into the freezing Han River because their life insurance policies were the only assets they had left. Men exactly like his old mentor, whose brains he had effectively blown out with a fountain pen.


“I… I never thought about them,” Arthur wept, the tears tracking through the deep, weathered wrinkles of his cheek, hanging at the precipice of his chin, and dropping onto his pajama-covered knee. He squeezed his eyes shut, but the darkness offered no refuge. The ghosts were inside the house.


“They most likely didn’t make it, did they?” Arthur sobbed, his hands trembling violently. The Parkinson's tremor was entirely overshadowed by his psychological collapse. “The families… the children. We took their homes, and I bought another yacht. Why? Why didn't I ever stop and help them? I had so much... I had so much, and I just let them drown.”


He looked up at Yeongon, his expression a devastating portrait of agonizing, unfamiliar empathy. The sheer scale of his own monstrosity was finally visible to him. The weight of his own sudden humanity—a muscle he hadn't flexed in half a century—was crushing him, compressing his lungs until he could barely draw breath.


“It’s alright, darling,” Yeongon said softly, sliding a thick leather-bound folio with his favorite pen as a bow on top, across the glass table toward him. Resting perfectly on top of the documents was his favorite heavy Montblanc fountain pen—the exact same weapon he had used to ruin them.


“We can fix it,” she murmured, her voice a soothing, melodic hum. “You have the power to balance the scales before you go. You just have to sign.” She had a moderately hard time keeping from smiling with satisfaction and pride. Consuming his soul and stripping him of everything after, she felt her chest swell with pride and couldn’t help but think how she can’t wait to come up with just a set up for the celestials.


Arthur didn't hesitate. He didn't call his lawyers and he didn't read the addenda. He picked up the heavy Montblanc pen and began signing away the Woo Telecom empire with a feverish need like that of a man drinking his first glass of water after he spent his entire life in a desert. He transferred the liquid assets to a series of blind trusts controlled exclusively by his new wife, and earmarked the remaining corporate holdings for mass philanthropic liquidation.


Yeongon watched the ink flow. She could feel the tingling of her scale in response to her self acknowledgement. Now she was set up for the life of ninety-nine more. She took another sip of her tea, her dark eyes flicking momentarily toward the reinforced glass of the window, specifically to the exact coordinates on the ridge where she knew a man with a laser microphone was having a very bad morning.


In the subterranean command center beneath the Woo corporate tower, the glow of the encrypted video feed painted Jin Woo’s face in a sickly, pale light. On the monitor, Arthur Woo—the ruthless patriarch—was weeping as he signed away the telecom empire.


Jin didn't just watch; he vibrated with a pure, unadulterated rage. He slammed his fists down onto the slab of the obsidian desk so hard the structural glass of his monitor shuddered. For a fleeting, pathetic moment, his immaculate posture broke. He hunched over the glowing screen, his spine curling inward like a starving, corrupted creature desperate to protect its singular, precious prize.


He had not earned this empire by sitting comfortably in boardrooms. Like his grandfather, Jin had been exiled in his youth. But where Arthur had been shielded by checkbooks and trust funds, Jin had actively severed his own safety nets. He had demanded the world beat him into submission, and the world had happily obliged. He had slept on freezing concrete, been spat on by the public, and had his ribs cracked in dark alleyways by desperate citizens who had seen right through his earliest, clumsy attempts at systemic corruption. He had starved. He had bled. He had allowed the harshest environments on the planet to forge him into a weapon sharp enough to take the Woo throne.


And now, some pale, nameless parasite in a white dress was stealing his birthright with a cup of tea and a whisper.
Jin forced his spine back into a rigid, immaculate line. He adjusted the cuffs of his bespoke suit, burying the feral trauma back under layers of cold corporate sociopathy, and keyed the secure comms line to Hawaii.


On the secondary screen, Elias Thorne looked terrible. The intelligence contractor was broadcasting from his mobile command center. The heavy bags under Elias’s eyes were bruised purple, and the man’s usual cynical, relaxed posture had completely evaporated.


“I told you to watch her, Thorne,” Jin snapped, his voice dropping into a dangerous, icy register that betrayed none of his previous panic. 


“She gave him a bath and then he passed out, but then she looked at me, dead in my eyes man.” Elias shook with unnerving fear remembering the cold ice that washed over him.


“You’re telling me she noticed a passive laser mic from a mile and a half away? In the dark?” Jin said sharply.


“She didn't just notice it, Jin. She addressed me by name. She knew my exact heart rate.”


“She’s bluffing. She has a counter-surveillance team sweeping the perimeter. They spotted your thermal signature. She’s a con artist, Elias. And now a highly funded one.” He murmured the last bit.


Elias let out a harsh, dry laugh that sounded like sandpaper against wood. “I am telling you, as a man who has tracked hundreds…NO thousand of people, some in three different war zones, she is NO human.”


Jin pinched the bridge of his nose exasperatedly. “Give me data, Elias. Not bullshit. Data.”


On the screen, Elias typed a command. A file transfer notification popped up on Jin’s terminal. “Open the spectrogram file,” Elias commanded. “I ran her vocal signature through the analyzer. Look at the waveform at the 0:18 mark. Right before she looked right at me through the glass and whispered into my secured mic!”


Jin clicked the file. A green, jagged graphical interface filled his screen.


“Look a human larynx produces a single point of origin for a sound wave, This is why beatboxers have to time their sounds one after the other and not in sync with the rest of the sounds. ONE LARNYX!” Elias explained, his voice deteriorating into clinical detachment. “What you are looking at is a simultaneous dual-frequency emission. She is projecting a clean sine wave and a twelve-hertz acoustic flutter at the exact same time. The structural biology required to make that sound does not exist in mammals, Jin. So unless there’s a rare disorder where you have two Larynxses-es,” He stumbled through his words. “WITHOUT having a conjoined twin, then she’s not human.”


Jin stared at the green lines. The math was right there. As a man who worshipped data, he felt the floor of his reality suddenly drop a few inches.


“What did she do to my grandfather?” Jin asked, his voice losing its corporate edge, dropping into something quiet and cold.


“She lobotomized his ego,” Elias replied. “I monitored his vitals. She didn't torture him. She spoke to him, used that auditory trigger, and his cognitive defenses evaporated. By the time you get the legal paperwork, Arthur Woo will have given her everything.”


Jin leaned back in his leather chair and rubbed his temples much harder than he should have. The empire was gone, all the pain, sweat and tears he spent to take over Woo Telecom all gone. As he browsed at the acoustic data on the screen, a new, much darker realization took hold.


“If she isn't human,” Jin murmured, his eyes narrowing, “then she doesn't care about the money. The money is just a vehicle.”


“For what?”


“Access,” Jin said, pulling up the guest list for the upcoming Woo Telecom annual shareholder summit—a private, ultra-exclusive gathering of fifty of the wealthiest, most corrupt institutional power brokers in Asia. “She used my grandfather to get the security clearance. She’s not stopping with him, Elias. She’s scaling up. Listen, I remember from watching old plays in Seoul, my dad took me to one where a Yeongno is a proto-dragon and consumes the rich. Sort of a warning to the elite to watch your step.”


“You think she’s a fucking dragon?!”


“Proto-Dragon called Yeongno.” Jin said matter-of-factly and looked back at the screen. “Don't lose her, Thorne. I’m wiring you an additional two million. I want to know exactly what she hunts next.”


“What are you going to do?” Elias looked over his shoulder to Jin incredulously.


“I’m going to catch it and prove what she is.” Jin said as a disturbing smile began to eat the lower half of his face.
