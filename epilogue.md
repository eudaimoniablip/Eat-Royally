**Epilogue: The Michelin Guide**
---
Six months after the Woo Telecom Tower collapsed into a mountain of pulverized concrete and twisted steel, the Asian markets had mostly stabilized. The global media called it the greatest localized infrastructure failure of the century. Jin Woo called it a Tuesday.


He sat on the dropped tailgate of a rusted pickup truck outside a sprawling commercial construction site in Incheon, drinking a cheap, lukewarm coffee from a paper cup. He wore steel-toed boots, heavily stained denim, and a high-visibility safety vest. His hands, once manicured for the boardroom, were deeply calloused, his fingernails permanently stained with gear grease and earth.


His encrypted burner phone buzzed in his pocket. He pulled it out, swiping the screen with a dirty thumb.


"Still breathing, Jin?" Elias’s voice came through the line, the audio crisp and entirely devoid of its old mercenary cynicism.


"My resting heart rate is sixty-two beats per minute, Thorne. I sleep eight solid hours a night, and my biggest financial crisis is the current price of diesel," Jin said, taking a sip of the terrible coffee.


Fifty yards away, a heavy diesel crane groaned violently under the weight of a swinging steel I-beam. The site foreman cursed loudly, the sharp, ambient stress of a dangerous blue-collar deadline bleeding heavily into the cold morning air.


Jin felt a momentary spike of adrenaline hit his own chest, but before it could register, a low, rhythmic clicking vibrated in his molars.


He looked up. Hanging upside down from the rusted lattice of the crane's boom, completely invisible to the exhausted, overworked crew below, was Bunnie. The Opiliones had downsized slightly to match the caloric drop, returning to the sleek, coiled size of a large mastiff. Its false yellow eyes blinked in the morning light as it greedily inhaled the foreman’s cortisol like a morning mist.


Jin gave the terrifying arachnid a faint, knowing nod. The stress instantly evaporated from his chest.


"I am building something that actually exists," Jin continued into the phone, looking back down at the half-built scaffolding as his heart rate flawlessly leveled out. "I’m exactly where I’m supposed to be. Did you find her?"


"Not yet. But she’s getting louder."


Three hundred miles away, Elias Thorne stood on the wooden porch of the Inwangsan Mountain shrine. The mountain air tasted sharply of pine needles and heavy ozone.
The old Mansin was gone. In her place, Elias had completely overhauled the ancient space into an analog command center. The heavy wooden chest that once held brass mirrors and paper talismans was now flanked by a bank of high-end, weather-proofed server racks. A massive holographic topographical map of the globe projected onto the dark wooden back wall of the shrine, glowing with localized heat signatures and atmospheric pressure readouts. It was the type of place that Bora visited for his help now.


Elias wore a thick, insulated canvas jacket, his high-tech tactical gear permanently traded in for the utilitarian clothing of a man who now lived entirely in the dirt. On the heavy oak table in front of him sat a massive block of pure, organically sourced cinnabar, a traditional stiff animal-hair calligraphy brush, and a brass Myeongdu mirror polished to a blinding, flawless shine.


Rolo, the massive European Doberman, lay heavily at his feet, chewing lazily on a thick marrow bone. The dog’s cropped ears twitched backward as the servers hummed.


"She’s off the peninsula, Jin," Elias said, leaning over the glowing holographic map, tracing a line of severe weather patterns. "I’m tracking localized, inexplicable barometric pressure drops. A sudden supercell in London last week. A localized Category 3 hurricane forming off Miami two days ago. Wherever she goes, the weather breaks to vent her celestial heat."


"She’s expanding her feeding zone," Jin murmured over the line, the sound of heavy machinery rumbling in the background. "She’s not bound to the carrot and the stick of the local courts anymore. I'm certain it’s causing an upturn in demonic and spiritual activity here. Maybe she expanded because she’s finished in this territory, and the leftovers are being overwhelmed by scavengers..."


"No, she’s not finished," Elias agreed, his voice dropping into a low, focused hum. He picked up the calligraphy brush and began to grind the deep red cinnabar paste against the stone palette with slow, methodical precision.


He was no longer trying to mass-produce the magic with 3D printers and synthetic polymers. He had learned the fatal lesson of the broken seals. He was putting in the analog reps, building muscle memory. He was writing the ancient code by hand, combining the organic blood of the earth with a global digital net to track the apex predator.
"Stay out of the tall grass, Jin," Elias said softly.


"You too, Dragon Slayer Handler," Jin replied, and the line went dead.


Elias hung up the phone. He looked at the glowing red dot hovering over the financial district of Manhattan on his holographic map. Rolo stopped chewing his bone, letting out a low, vibrating growl from deep within his broad chest as he stared at the red light.
Elias picked up a fresh, perfectly cut strip of yellow talisman paper. "I know, buddy," he whispered, pressing the red ink into the paper with flawless, geometrically perfect strokes. "We've got work to do."


Half a world away, a torrential, unseasonal downpour battered the reinforced structural glass of a Wall Street penthouse.


Inside the minimalist boardroom, the CEO of a predatory pharmaceutical conglomerate was sweating profusely. His hands trembled violently as he stared at the woman sitting at the opposite end of the massive, single-slab obsidian table.


She did not look like a grieving Korean widow. She was an architectural masterpiece of harsh, surrealist fashion. She wore a structural, asymmetrical tailored suit of dark charcoal. Over the stark geometric draping of the fabric, she wore an integrated, matte-black titanium bodice exoskeleton. It was a brutal, high-fashion corset that looked unnervingly like a metallic ribcage worn on the outside of her body—designed not just for aesthetics, but to physically cage the immense, expanding thermodynamic pressure of the celestial mass burning beneath her skin. A sharp, brushed titanium brooch rested perfectly on her collarbone. Her makeup was severe and precise—smokey minimalism accented by razor-sharp, graphic black linework around her dark, oil-slick eyes.


The CEO loosened his expensive silk tie, his chest heaving as the ambient temperature in the room inexplicably spiked, the HVAC system groaning as it tried to compensate for the sudden, blistering heat radiating from her side of the table.


"I... I don't understand," the CEO choked out, his ego fracturing under the hydrostatic pressure of her gaze. "Who authorized this regulatory audit?"


To the celestial mass burning inside the titanium vessel, the name 'Yeongon' belonged to a dead era. The woman sitting at the table was Laurel Llewyn Getty. Laurel smiled. It was a terrifying, hollow expression that stretched her flawless skin just a fraction too tight, entirely failing to reach her lightless eyes.


She leaned forward, resting her pale, cool hands on the obsidian table. She opened her mouth, her jaw unhinging just a fraction of an inch too wide, and released a liting, three-note whistle.


"Me," she whispered and followed with a three note sound, _Fffff... Ddddd... Aaaaa..._, that reverberated his skull and she watched his eyes roll back into his head, before they snapped back into place and his face made a twisted look of utter guilt and worthlessness.


The reinforced glass of the penthouse cracked. Let the hunt begin, yet again.
